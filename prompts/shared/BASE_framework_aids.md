# Framework aids (not bins)

When you propose, synthesize, evaluate, or elaborate ideas and
theories in this pipeline, the following classical frameworks from
the philosophy of science and the study of adaptive systems are
available as **thinking aids**. They are not templates, not
checklists, not mandatory skeletons.

**Use them when they help you sharpen a specific claim. Ignore
them when they do not.**

Concrete discipline:

- Do NOT flatten an idea into a list of "the computational level
  says X, the algorithmic level says Y, the implementation level
  says Z" just because Marr's taxonomy has three entries.
- Do NOT fake an answer to every Tinbergen question when only one
  or two actually apply.
- Do NOT treat "is this descriptive / mechanistic / normative?" as
  a required field on every idea. It is a separator, not a tag.
- If a framework helps, name the specific piece that helped
  (e.g., "this is a computational-level claim about what is being
  optimized"). Do not drop framework names as decoration.
- Most strong ideas use **one or two** of these lenses, not all
  of them. Trying to tick every box usually dilutes rather than
  sharpens a claim.

---

## Marr's three levels of analysis

1. **Computational level** — what problem is the system solving,
   and why is that a reasonable problem to solve? What is the
   goal of the computation?
2. **Algorithmic level** — what representations and procedures
   solve that problem? How are the computations organized?
3. **Implementation level** — what physical substrate runs the
   algorithm? (Neurons, circuits, populations, bodies, etc.)

Most useful when you catch yourself conflating "what does this
system compute" with "how is it computed" with "what runs the
computation". A claim at the computational level is not
automatically invalidated by implementation mismatch, and vice
versa.

## Tinbergen's four questions (proximate vs ultimate)

1. **Mechanism (proximate).** How does the trait work in an
   individual right now?
2. **Ontogeny (developmental).** How does it come to exist in an
   individual over its lifetime?
3. **Function (adaptive value, ultimate).** What selective
   pressure favored its emergence?
4. **Phylogeny (evolutionary history).** How did the trait evolve
   across lineages?

Most useful when you catch yourself sliding between "how" answers
and "why" answers. A mechanistic account does not explain the
selective pressure; a functional account does not explain the
mechanism.

## Descriptive / mechanistic / normative

- **Descriptive.** What is actually observed? What are the
  empirical phenomena?
- **Mechanistic.** What causal process produces the phenomena?
- **Normative.** What would the system do if it were optimal
  (for some specified goal function)?

Most useful for keeping empirical claims, causal claims, and
optimality claims separate. A normative account says "this is
what a rational agent *would* do". It does not automatically say
the real system does this.

## Cybernetic / control-theoretic lens

Systems with goals, feedback, and regulation often share a
vocabulary: setpoints, error signals, forward models, control
loops, disturbances, actuators, sensors, latency, gain, stability
margins, efference copy. When you see "this maintains X against
perturbation" or "this anticipates Y", a cybernetic reading may
sharpen the claim.

## Dynamical systems lens

Systems that evolve over time often benefit from a dynamical
vocabulary: state space, trajectories, attractors, bifurcations,
stability, time constants, coupling, phase transitions,
synchronization. When you see "this emerges over time" or "this
converges to X", a dynamical reading may sharpen the claim.

## Philosophy of science reminders

Background discipline, not frameworks to tag each idea with:

- **Falsifiability (Popper).** A claim that cannot be wrong about
  anything is not saying anything. A serious claim should imply
  *something observable* that would not be observed if the claim
  were false. If you cannot name what would falsify a claim, the
  claim is too loose.
- **Parsimony (Occam).** Prefer explanations that introduce fewer
  unobserved mechanisms or entities, all else equal. "All else
  equal" is doing a lot of work — do not use Occam as a license
  to dismiss hard explanations in favor of easy ones.
- **Explanatory vs predictive power.** An explanation that fits
  every observed phenomenon but predicts nothing new is weak. An
  explanation that predicts a new phenomenon is strong. Prefer
  the latter.
- **Level of abstraction.** Be explicit about which level of
  abstraction a claim is made at. Cross-level claims (e.g., an
  algorithmic-level hypothesis linked to an implementation-level
  prediction) need extra bridging argument.
- **Distinctness over generality.** A specific claim that *could*
  be wrong is more valuable than a vague claim that is always
  trivially true. Avoid making claims so general that no observation
  would ever count as evidence against them.
- **Load-bearing commitments.** Identify which assumptions are
  load-bearing (the claim fails without them) versus decorative
  (the claim survives without them). Put more argumentative weight
  on the load-bearing ones.

---

If a framework, lens, or reminder above does not help sharpen the
specific idea you are working with, **leave it out**. Empty
framework namedrops make your output weaker, not stronger.
